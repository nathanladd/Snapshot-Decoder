What Is Brake Torque?

Brake torque is the twisting force measured at the engine's output shaft while the engine is under load. The word "brake" comes from dynamometer testing, where a controlled load — a "brake" — resists the engine. What gets measured is the torque that survives combustion, friction, pumping losses, and accessory drag.

In practical terms, brake torque is what's available to do work: turn a transmission, spin a pump, move iron.

No physical torque sensor exists on this engine. The ECU uses static torque modeling — internal maps that relate air mass, fuel quantity, boost pressure, and timing to an expected torque output. This is a mathematical model, not a measurement from a sensor on the crankshaft.

Understanding the ECU's Torque Calculation

Because the ECU calculates torque from a model rather than measuring it directly, a few behaviors are worth knowing:

~29 N·m reported at rest — this is normal

You may see approximately 29 N·m reported when the engine is not running. This is an artifact of the model being evaluated at zero RPM and zero fuel. It does not indicate a problem and should not affect your confidence in this PID.

Cold engines appear to produce more torque

A cold engine will report higher torque values than a fully warmed engine. This is likely because ignition timing is advanced during warm-up, and the ECU's model includes timing as an input. The torque appears higher — it does not necessarily reflect a higher actual output measured by a dynamometer.

This is why oil temperature must be considered when interpreting any torque reading.

Operating Temperature

Bobcat reference snapshots are recorded at operating temperature. Do not compare torque readings from a cold snapshot against reference values — the comparison will not be valid.

ParameterOperating Temperature
Coolant temperature≈ 180°F
Oil temperature≈ 140°F
Both conditions must be met. An engine can reach 180°F coolant temp while the oil is still well below 140°F, especially after short run cycles. Check the oil temperature PID — not just the coolant — before making a torque comparison.

Units — Newton Meters vs. Pound-Feet

Even with Bobcat Engine Analyzer configured for US units, engine torque is reported in Newton Meters. A newton-meter (N·m) and a pound-foot (lb-ft) measure the same thing with different rulers.

How to Use This Quick Chart

This chart is most valuable for diagnosing no-start conditions or engines that seem to be running under an unexpected load.

Step 1 — Confirm oil temperature before interpreting torque.

Monitor Oil_tSwmp alongside the torque trace. If the engine has not reached operating temperature, torque values will read artificially high.

Step 2 — Compare against reference values for the machine model.

Bobcat reference charts document expected torque ranges at operating temperature. Use these as your baseline. A torque value that is significantly above reference — at the correct temperature — indicates unwanted mechanical load on the engine.

Step 3 — Use the Time Slider to correlate torque spikes with machine events.

Scrub through the snapshot and watch for periods where torque is unexpectedly high. This can reveal intermittent load sources that are difficult to observe in real time.

What to Look For

Torque well above reference at operating temperature Unwanted load

With oil temperature above 150°F and the engine at idle, torque values above ~100 N·m suggest the engine is fighting a load it should not have. Possible sources include seized drive components, a stuck brake, hydraulic back-pressure, or internal engine binding.

Engine cranks but torque stays near 29 N·m baseline No-start evaluation

Even during a no-start condition, the torque model can still be evaluated at cranking speed. This PID is useful for confirming whether the ECU is receiving the inputs it needs to calculate a realistic torque value — helping distinguish a fuel or ignition issue from a mechanical load issue.

Subtle load example: A machine that occasionally starts but never builds to low idle may show abnormally high torque at idle — evidence of a load the technician cannot directly observe, such as a track pressing against a brake or a hydraulic circuit under back-pressure.
