PID Details

P_L_Rail_pres_raw — Actual Rail Pressure

The measured fuel pressure in the common rail, as reported by the rail pressure sensor. This is the real-time pressure available for injection — what the fuel system is actually delivering.

RPC_Rail_pressure_dmnd — Demanded Rail Pressure

The ECU's target fuel pressure, calculated from engine speed, load, and temperature. The ECU adjusts the Inlet Metering Valve (IMV) on the high-pressure pump to achieve this target.

Under healthy conditions, actual and demanded pressure track closely together. A persistent gap between them points to a fuel system problem.

FQD_Chkd_inj_fuel_dmnd — Total Fuel Injection Quantity

Use this PID to identify steady-state periods in the snapshot — times when the engine is running at consistent speed and load. Look for sections of the chart where fuel injection quantity is flat or consistent.

Rail pressure should only be evaluated during steady state. Transient periods (acceleration, deceleration) cause both actual and demanded pressure to fluctuate naturally.

Critical Thresholds — Bobcat V1 Engines

Minimum cranking pressure: Rail pressure must reach 1,450 PSI before the ECU will begin fuel delivery. The engine will not start if this threshold is not met during cranking.
Maximum allowable gap: If the difference between actual and demanded rail pressure exceeds 2,175 PSI for 5 seconds, the ECU will shut down fuel injection.

How to Use This Quick Chart

Step 1 — Load your snapshot and open the Rail Pressure Quick Chart.

Actual and demanded rail pressure plot on the left axis. Total fuel injection quantity plots on the right axis.

Step 2 — Use total fuel injection quantity to find steady-state periods.

Look for sections where the fuel quantity line is flat. These are the valid windows for evaluating pressure control. Ignore pressure fluctuations that coincide with changes in fuel delivery.

Step 3 — Compare actual vs. demanded pressure during steady state.

Use the Vertical Cursor, Rulers, or Time Scrubber to read exact values. The two lines should track closely. Calculate the gap — a persistent difference points to a fuel system fault.

Identifying Problems

Large gap — Actual below Demanded Supply problem

The fuel system cannot keep up with demand. Possible causes:

  • Fuel supply restriction (filter, lift pump, low tank)
  • IMV stuck open or IMV circuit fault
  • High-pressure pump worn or failing
  • Injectors leaking or blocked (return fuel reducing net rail pressure)
Actual above Demanded during steady state IMV / circuit issue

The system is over-pressurizing — the IMV is not reducing pressure as commanded. Possible causes:

  • IMV stuck closed or mechanically binding
  • No 12V supply to the IMV — check the engine cross-over connector
  • Blocked injector(s) preventing fuel from returning

Tips

Use Rulers to calculate the gap. Place two rulers on the actual and demanded lines at the same time point to read exact values and quickly calculate the pressure difference.
Zoom into steady-state periods. Transient pressure swings during load changes are normal. Zoom in on flat sections of the fuel delivery line for reliable pressure control analysis.
